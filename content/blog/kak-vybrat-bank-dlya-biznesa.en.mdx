## Why choosing a bank matters more than it seems

Many foreigners treat the bank as a formality: "I'll open wherever, as long as I have an account." In practice this is one of the most expensive mistakes. A bank is not just a place to store money but your partner on every transaction: it processes payments, approves currency deals, and is responsible for speed and for whether your transfer gets stuck on review.

For a local company the difference between banks is less noticeable. But the moment a non-resident founder, foreign revenue and international payments enter the picture, banks start behaving very differently. One bank opens an account in a few days and processes SWIFT calmly; another spends weeks requesting documents and slows down every currency payment. So the choice should be made before opening an account, not after the first refusal.

## How to choose a business bank in Uzbekistan: six criteria for a foreigner?

Comparing banks "by feel" is pointless. It is easier to break the decision into concrete criteria and go through each one. Below are six parameters that genuinely affect how a foreign company operates, and why each matters specifically for a non-resident.

<IconGrid>
  <Feature icon="globe" title="Experience with non-residents">Whether the bank has an established procedure for foreign founders and a clear document list — this lowers the risk of refusal.</Feature>
  <Feature icon="wallet" title="Currency operations and SWIFT">How convenient export-import settlements are, whether there is direct SWIFT, and what the timelines and fees on currency look like.</Feature>
  <Feature icon="bank" title="Online banking">The quality of online banking for legal entities: whether you can manage the account remotely, and the mobile app and interface.</Feature>
  <Feature icon="scale" title="Fees and commissions">The cost of opening and servicing, plus fees for payments and conversion — but this is not the main or only criterion.</Feature>
  <Feature icon="rocket" title="Speed of opening">How quickly the bank clears compliance and opens the account — for a business where deals are urgent, this is critical.</Feature>
  <Feature icon="shield" title="Transparent compliance">Predictable requirements and review times matter more than a pretty fee schedule: you know in advance what is expected.</Feature>
</IconGrid>

Next we go through each criterion in more detail — with a focus on what exactly to check and what to ask the bank before opening an account.

### Experience with non-residents

This is criterion number one. A bank that regularly opens accounts for foreign companies already knows which documents are needed, how to handle a passport translation and how to describe an ownership structure. A bank without that experience will learn on you: requesting documents in pieces, asking again, clearing each step with its compliance team. A simple question up front — "how many accounts for foreign founders did you open last year" — saves weeks.

### Currency operations and SWIFT

If you receive revenue from abroad or pay foreign contractors, the currency block is decisive. Clarify: does the bank have direct SWIFT access or do payments go through a correspondent bank (this affects timelines and fees), how quickly are currency operations approved, and what documents are needed for export-import contracts. The order of currency operations in the country is set by the regulator, but the speed and convenience within those rules differ from bank to bank.

### Online banking and remote management

For a non-resident who is not based in Tashkent permanently, the quality of online banking is a matter of survival, not comfort. Check: can you sign payments remotely, is there a mobile app, in which languages is the interface, and how does support work. Good online banking lets you run the company from anywhere in the world; bad online banking forces you to fly in for every signature.

### Fees and commissions

Fees matter, but they are often overrated. Account opening and servicing, payment charges, currency conversion fees and SWIFT-transfer commissions all add up to the real cost. But chasing the cheapest bank is dangerous: savings on a fee are easily wiped out by losses from slow compliance or a stuck payment. Fees also change regularly, so you should check them on the specific bank's website rather than in third-party reviews.

### Speed of opening and compliance

Speed is not about "opening in an hour" but about predictability. For a business where deals do not wait, the difference between a bank that opens an account in a few days and one that drags it out for weeks can cost a contract. Ask directly: what is the average time to open an account for a foreign company, and what does it take to go faster.

### Transparency and support

The last, underrated criterion is how clearly the bank communicates. A precise document list, timelines stated up front, support in a language you understand, and a dedicated manager for legal entities all turn account opening from a lottery into a managed process.

## What types of banks are there in Uzbekistan: state, private and foreign-capital?

Banks in Uzbekistan are conveniently split into three groups. Each has its own character and strengths. Understanding this map helps narrow the choice even before comparing specific fees.

<CompareGrid>
  <CompareCard title="State banks" badge="scale and stability">
    Large banks with wide branch networks and a big market share. The pluses are reliability, scale and developed infrastructure for domestic settlements. The minuses for a foreigner — processes are sometimes more formalised, and decisions can be slower.
  </CompareCard>
  <CompareCard title="Private banks" badge="flexibility and digital">
    Commercial banks that compete actively on service. The pluses are flexibility, strong digital products, convenient mobile apps and often faster onboarding. The minuses — banks vary, and the quality of work with non-residents depends heavily on the specific bank.
  </CompareCard>
  <CompareCard title="Foreign-capital banks" badge="international focus" highlight>
    Banks with foreign capital or subsidiaries of overseas groups. The pluses for a non-resident are familiar standards, convenient international settlements and experience with foreign clients. The minuses — often stricter compliance and careful source-of-funds checks.
  </CompareCard>
</CompareGrid>

<Callout type="info" title="The bank type is a guide, not a verdict">
The split is rough: a strong private bank may work with foreigners better than any state bank, and a particular state bank may have an excellent currency department. The type sets the general logic, but the decision is always made on the specific bank and your set of criteria.
</Callout>

### How the bank type relates to your model

A rough guide: if you run export-import trade with active international payments, look first at banks with a strong currency block and SWIFT — often foreign-capital or large private banks. If you offer services, consulting or IT focused on remote management, online banking and predictable compliance come to the fore, where flexible private banks tend to shine. If scale, network and work with state counterparties matter most, state banks are the logical choice.

## Common mistakes when choosing a bank

Most problems with choosing a bank are predictable. Here is the honest picture: what helps you choose right, and what almost guarantees difficulties.

<ProsCons>
  <Pros title="How to choose right">

  - Compare 2–3 banks against a single checklist of criteria.
  - Define your model first (export, services, IT), then look for a bank to fit it.
  - Ask about experience with non-residents and the average time to open an account.
  - Check the currency block and SWIFT if you work with abroad.
  - Verify current fees on the bank's website before opening.

  </Pros>
  <Cons title="What to avoid">

  - Choosing a bank purely on the lowest fee.
  - Applying to a single bank at random with no comparison.
  - Ignoring online banking quality if you manage remotely.
  - Relying on fee figures from old articles.
  - Underestimating compliance and trying to push an opaque structure through.

  </Cons>
</ProsCons>

<Callout type="warning" title="The most common mistake foreigners make">
Choosing a bank on fees and convenient location while forgetting compliance and currency. For a non-resident, predictability matters most: a bank that explains requirements clearly and processes international payments calmly is ultimately more profitable than the cheapest one.
</Callout>
